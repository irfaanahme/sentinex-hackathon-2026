 
H4. The Medication Safety Second Pair of Eyes 
Real-world pain: A patient in a district hospital is on medicines prescribed by three diﬀerent 
doctors across two years. Nobody has the full list. Dangerous interactions, duplicate therapies, 
and doses unsafe for the patient's kidney function slip through — especially for elderly patients 
on 8+ drugs. 
The problem: Given a patient's consolidated medication list and basic clinical context, ﬂag 
safety risks with a clear severity and a speciﬁc suggested action for the prescriber. 
What we provide: 
 
 A simpliﬁed drug reference of ~500 medicines: generic name, class, common brands, renal-
adjustment ﬂag, pregnancy category 
 A curated interaction table (~2,000 pairs) with severity 
 5,000 synthetic patient records: age, weight, eGFR (kidney function), pregnancy status, 
diagnoses, and a medication list including handwritten-style brand/generic mix and 
misspellings 
 Hidden labels: the set of true safety issues per patient, and 300 deliberately seeded near-
miss cases 
Assumptions students may make: 
 
 The medication list may be incomplete — the system must not claim the list is safe, only 
that no issue was found in what it saw 
 Brand-to-generic mapping must be resolved before checking (this is much of the real 
diﬃculty) 
 Reference tables are authoritative and complete for this exercise 
 No allergy history is available unless present in the record 
Desired output: 
 
1. A prioritised alert list per patient: issue type (interaction / duplicate therapy / renal dose / 
contraindication), severity, the drugs involved, and a concrete suggested action 
2. Alert fatigue is the real enemy and is scored. evaluate. py measures recall on serious issues 
and penalises low-severity alert volume. A system that ﬂags everything scores near zero — 
this is the central design lesson 
3. A fuzzy brand/generic resolution module with a reported match accuracy on the 
misspelled inputs 
4. Explicit "conﬁdence and limitations" text shown with every alert, plus a mandatory 
disclaimer that a clinician decides 
5. Recall on the 300 seeded near-misses, reported prominently 
 
Stretch: a deprescribing suggestion for polypharmacy patients, with the reasoning chain 
shown.
